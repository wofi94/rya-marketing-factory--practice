# Research and personas: Rya Rightsizing

## What the client sells, where, to whom
- **Sells:** senior rightsizing, downsizing and move management. Starts with a complimentary consultation (in-home or virtual), then three-hour blocks of time or more at $80/hr, with one family liaison throughout.
- **Where (this cycle):** West Vancouver. Wider service area is North & West Vancouver and the Sea to Sky corridor; office in Squamish.
- **To whom:** adult children helping an aging parent (usually first contact), the senior parent (final say), and referral partners who pass families to Rya.

## Buyer vs. user
| Role | Who | What they care about |
|---|---|---|
| Decides and pays | Adult child, often with siblings (assumption – validate with client) | A trustworthy local person, clear communication, a plan the family agrees on |
| Receives the service | Senior parent, usually a West Vancouver homeowner | Respect, their own pace, keeping what matters, staying in control of decisions |
| Influences the decision | Referral partners: realtors, estate lawyers, seniors' residences, nurses, social workers, community organizations | Recommending someone reliable who protects their own reputation |

## Market snapshot
Internal research only. Don't use these figures in copy without a linkable source.

- **Category:** senior move management. An established but small profession with low awareness; NASMM lists only three BC members (NASMM, 2026).
- **Local context:** 28.5% of West Vancouver residents are 65+ (vs. 19.0% nationally) and 56% of homeowners are mortgage-free, the highest in BC. 64% of homes are single-family, and over half of households have no children at home (District of West Vancouver, 2025; North Shore News).
- **Alternatives a buyer considers:**
  - Family does it themselves (siblings split the work)
  - A general moving or junk-removal company
  - A larger move management firm with set packages
  - Waiting or putting it off (assumption – validate with client)
- **Trigger moments:**
  - Parent decides to move to senior living or a smaller home
  - Family home is being prepared for sale
  - Settling a parent's estate after a loss
  - Siblings can't agree on a plan, and the process stalls
  - A change in a parent's mobility or health (assumption – validate with client)
- **Visibility gap:** Rya is not on Google's first page for four non-brand search terms (team check, October 18, 2026), so referrals matter more than search in this cycle.

## Persona 1: Michelle, the adult child caught between a hesitant parent and a growing to-do list
- **Who she is:** Early 50s, working full time, with a parent in a West Vancouver family home. Lives outside the North Shore or has a demanding schedule (assumption – validate with client). One of two or three siblings.
- **Situation right now:** Michelle can see that the family home is becoming too much for her parent, but her parent isn't ready to talk about rightsizing. Every time she raises it, the conversation ends quickly. Meanwhile, the siblings each want something different, and she has become the default organizer.
- **Why her parent hesitates (assumption – validate with client):**
  - *The home is part of who they are:* decades of family memories, pride in being a West Vancouver homeowner, and, for some, grief for a spouse whose presence is still in the house.
  - *Fear of losing control:* worry that the decision is being made for them, that moving smaller means admitting they're less capable, and uncertainty about where they would go next.
  - *The task feels too big:* sorting decades of belongings is overwhelming, choosing who gets meaningful items is emotional, and limited mobility makes the physical work feel impossible.
  - *How Rya responds:* the parent decides (dignity), nothing moves faster than they're ready (your pace, your plan), and the goal is keeping what matters, not simply having less (rightsizing, not just downsizing).
- **Goal:** Help her parent reach their own decision, at their own pace, and then follow one plan the whole family can accept, carried out by someone she trusts.
- **Fears:**
  - *Practical:* not knowing where to start; not being able to take time off; the house becoming harder to manage while the family waits; letting a stranger into her parent's home.
  - *Emotional:* guilt about not being there in person; worry that pushing will hurt her relationship with her parent; feeling stuck between respecting her parent's wishes and worrying about them.
  - *Social:* conflict with siblings; being seen as the one "forcing" the move.
- **What reassures her:**
  - Her parent stays in charge of every decision, and nothing happens before they're ready
  - A complimentary consultation, in-home or virtual, that feels like a conversation rather than a commitment
  - One local liaison who keeps the whole family informed
  - Clear, transparent pricing in three-hour blocks of time or more
  - Belongings going to local donations, with as little as possible going to landfill
- **Where she looks for help:** Google and AI search ("how to talk to my parent about downsizing," "downsizing help West Vancouver"), her parent's realtor or residence, friends who have been through it, Facebook and Instagram (assumption – validate with client).
- **In her words:**
  - "Every time I bring up the house, Mom changes the subject."
  - "I don't want to push her, but I worry about her managing those stairs."
  - "My brother thinks we should just hire movers and be done with it."
  - "I don't even know who you call for something like this."
- **Message cue:** Your parent can move at their own pace, and you don't have to navigate this alone. We start with a conversation, not a commitment, and keep your parent at the centre of every decision.

## Persona 2: David, the West Vancouver realtor (referral partner)
- **Who he is:** An established North Shore realtor who often lists family homes for older homeowners and their adult children. Estate lawyers share many of the same needs; see the notes below.
- **Situation right now:** A listing is waiting on a home full of decades of belongings. The family is stressed, the timeline is tight, and his client keeps asking him who can help.
- **Goal:** A reliable person he can recommend who gets the home ready, keeps the family calm and protects his relationship with the client.
- **Fears:**
  - *Practical:* the listing date slipping; a provider who doesn't show up or communicate.
  - *Emotional:* watching his clients struggle through a hard transition.
  - *Social:* a bad referral damaging his reputation and future referrals.
- **What reassures him:**
  - Lisa's background: 20+ years in education and student support, and board roles with Making Connections Community Society and Squamish Seniors Living Society
  - Real testimonials: Tanya Ziyone's 20-acre estate and Bronwyn Preece's storage locker
  - One point of contact for the family
  - Local donations and landfill diversion
  - A simple referral kit he can hand to clients
- **Where he looks for help:** Peer referrals, Rotary Club of Lions Gate, Toastmasters, brokerage networks, and downsizing seminars where realtors, advisors and families gather (Amica Senior Lifestyles, 2025).
- **In his words:**
  - "The house won't show well until it's cleared, and the family is overwhelmed."
  - "I need someone I can trust inside my clients' parents' home."
  - "Can they work around the listing date?"
- **Message cue:** A kind, organized partner you can confidently hand a family to, so the home is ready and your clients feel cared for.
- **Estate lawyer notes:** Same need for trust and reliability. The difference is that the family is grieving, so the tone is quiet, tender and patient (brand book section 7).

## The person at the centre: the senior parent
Not a separate persona this cycle, but every message must respect them. They want respect, their own pace and to keep what matters. They give the final yes. Never describe them as frail or "elderly," and never treat their belongings as "junk."
